# Suitability & alternates

Goal: for every candidate place/activity, say plainly who it's good for, weighed against the actual group — not a generic "family-friendly" label — and give an alternate wherever it leaves part of the group out.

## Evaluating fit

Look at the youngest and oldest person in the group as the two edges to check against. A hike that's fine for a 35-year-old solo traveler needs no caveat. A hike being considered for a group spanning ages 1 to 16 needs a real assessment: is it a stroller-friendly paved loop, or a multi-mile trail an infant can't do and a 16-year-old will find boring?

Write the fit as a note, not a binary pass/fail — something like "great for the older kids, likely too much for the toddler" is more useful than silently excluding the item or silently including it as if it works for everyone. The point isn't to filter the list down to only-universal items — a smaller, wide-open group (like a solo trip or a same-age friend group) won't need much of this at all, and that's fine; don't manufacture caveats where none exist.

## Suggesting alternates

When an item doesn't fit part of the group, suggest something comparable — similar category, similar location — that the excluded members could do instead, ideally something the rest of the group could also enjoy or that runs in parallel. If you genuinely can't find a reasonable alternate, say so directly ("no direct alternate found nearby") rather than forcing a weak substitute just to fill the field.

## When group composition is incomplete

If you weren't given full ages for everyone, you should have already asked during intake. If the user couldn't provide it, you're working from a general-audience assumption — carry that forward and make sure the assumption is visible in the final output, not just implied.
